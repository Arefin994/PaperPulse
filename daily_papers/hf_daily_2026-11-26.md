## 1. Lazy Agents Answer Against Their Own Beliefs: Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Beliefs Teaches Them to Act on What They Believe
**Upvotes:** 👍 98 | **arXiv ID:** `2611.32884`

**Links:** [arXiv Page](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.32884) | [Download PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.32884.pdf) | [Hugging Face Discussion](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.32884)

### Abstract:
> AI agents working on long tasks often commit to an answer before they have enough evidence, a behavior called laziness. In our evaluations, they frequently commit even while their stated belief still names several possible answers. An outcome reward at the end of the episode scores only the final answer, so it cannot teach the agent when to commit, because a lucky guess receives the same reward as an answer backed by evidence. During training, however, the simulator can compute the exact posterior over the answer at every step and tell whether the evidence is already enough. We propose Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Beliefs (RLVB), which rewards the agent for taking further steps while this posterior still allows several answers and for committing once the evidence is sufficient. The reward needs no judge, critic or reward model, and it never reads the belief line the agent writes before each action. We also introduce Pro Worlds, a benchmark of visual puzzles where the agent views a large world through a small window. We evaluate three vision-language models on three benchmarks, two of which are never seen in training. RLVB improves the base model in all nine settings, by 17.1% on average. Under the outcome reward, GRPO and PPO fall below the base model on average. RLVB also outperforms PPO with a process reward from an LLM judge by 9.3% on average while using 1.6 times fewer GPU-hours. The trained agent answers with sufficient evidence in 51%, 12% and 74% of the episodes on the three benchmarks, against 8%, 0.4% and 36% for the base model. Its accuracy gain therefore comes from answering more often at high levels of evidence. The belief line shows how the agent reaches this evidence: it acts on its own belief. It commits at only 0.1 to 2.2% of the turns at which the evidence is insufficient and its line still names several answers, against 3 to 68% for the base model.

## 2. Nemotron-Labs-VisualCreation: Pixel as Code for Agentic Visual Creation
**Upvotes:** 👍 82 | **arXiv ID:** `2611.31358`

**Links:** [arXiv Page](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31358) | [Download PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.31358.pdf) | [Hugging Face Discussion](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.31358)

### Abstract:
> Visual creation demands expressive imagery together with precise control over text, layout, and structure. We introduce Nemotron-Labs-VisualCreation, an agentic framework that connects these capabilities through Pixel as Code. Raster images are represented by learned discrete visual tokens, while structured artifacts use explicit programs. A shared generation-and-rendering interface connects these representations to visual feedback across images, vector graphics, webpages, and documents. A Nemotron-native harness coordinates planning and raster candidate selection, with targeted repair for editable structured and hybrid artifacts. To support this interface, we develop Nemotron-Labs-Image-Tokenizer, the autoregressive raster generator Nemotron-Labs-Image-30B-A3B, and the structured-code model family Nemotron-Labs-VisionCode. On approximately 178K images spanning six visual domains, our tokenizer improves reconstruction PSNR from 26.08 to 29.57 dB over Emu3.5 IBQ. It also provides 7.13x faster encoding and 10.93x faster decoding at batch size one on A100. In standalone evaluation, one Nemotron-Labs-VisionCode variant surpasses Kimi K3.1 Pro on the aggregate across Chart2Code, MMSVG, Design2Code, and Image2Struct. At the system level, the Nemotron-native configuration raises GenEval2 Soft-TIFA GM from 31.71 to 52.56 using the same raster generator. With our best setting, Nemotron-Labs-VisualCreation achieves performance competitive with GPT Image 2 and Nano Banana Pro across most benchmarks with available comparisons.

## 3. LingSante MedGuard Technical Report
**Upvotes:** 👍 34 | **arXiv ID:** `2611.25509`

**Links:** [arXiv Page](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25509) | [Download PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.25509.pdf) | [Hugging Face Discussion](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.25509)

### Abstract:
> As medical AI is increasingly used for patient consultation and clinical decision support, identifying harmful, non-compliant, or ethically problematic responses is essential for responsible deployment. Assessing these risks requires checking clinical claims against relevant evidence, verifying calculations, and applying medical requirements under their intended conditions. We introduce LingSante MedGuard, a capability-enhanced guardrail that integrates supervision from a medical guardrail teacher model and six specialized medical capability experts into a unified model. The guardrail teacher supervises risk decisions and explanations across medical safety, compliance, and ethics, while the capability experts support medical rule understanding, numerical reasoning, multimodal hallucination detection, agentic safety verification, risk localization, and multimodal entity understanding. We consolidate these sources of supervision through offline supervised fine-tuning on teacher-generated demonstrations, followed by multi-teacher on-policy distillation using feedback on student-generated responses. We further introduce LingSante MedGuardBench, a benchmark comprising 11 risk-detection subsets spanning medical safety, compliance, and ethics. At 4B, 9B, and 27B scales, LingSante MedGuard achieves mean benchmark F1 scores of 82.3%, 82.4%, and 87.4%, respectively. The 4B model also achieves 73.9% mean accuracy across MedHallu and MedHALT-NOTA under retrieval-enabled evaluation. Additional evaluations of the verification components show improvements in evidence-supported consistency, source traceability, and rule-guided rationale quality across model scales. These results support the integration of specialized medical capabilities into a unified guardrail for medical risk assessment and evidence-based medical safety verification.

## 4. Sage-Med: Scaling Medical Multimodal Intelligence
**Upvotes:** 👍 27 | **arXiv ID:** `2611.34575`

**Links:** [arXiv Page](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34575) | [Download PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.34575.pdf) | [Hugging Face Discussion](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.34575)

### Abstract:
> Establishing effective scaling laws for medical multimodal large language models (MLLMs) remains a persistent and fundamental challenge. Our systematic evaluation shows that existing medical MLLMs tend to underperform frontier general-purpose MLLMs across a range of medical tasks, while scaling model size primarily improves text capabilities rather than multimodal perception, and enabling reasoning modes fails to close this visual gap. To investigate how medical multimodal intelligence scales in practice, we introduce Sage-Med-Preview, a 35B-parameter medical multimodal model with 3B activated parameters, built by combining stage-specific curation over 322.7 million candidate records with a progressive training curriculum spanning medical pretraining, staged supervised fine-tuning, and adaptive reinforcement learning. Across this curriculum, progressive supervised refinement and adaptive reinforcement learning yield consistent stage-wise gains, substantially advancing both specialized visual understanding and medical language capabilities. Evaluated against 77 competing models across 238 clinical tasks spanning 61 public and 13 private multi-center benchmarks in more than 40 modalities, Sage-Med-Preview achieves an average score of 58.26% on the 31 public multimodal benchmarks. It outperforms the state-of-the-art open-source medical model Hulu-Med-235B-A22B at 50.99% and the general scientific model Intern-S2-Preview at 52.24%, and approaches the advanced proprietary model GPT-6.2-Sol across multiple tasks such as comprehensive multimodal understanding, report generation, and visual grounding. Although substantial challenges remain in complex medical reasoning and multi-step clinical workflows, this report provides empirical evidence, actionable data and training recipes, and a rigorous benchmark toward scalable clinical multimodal intelligence.

## 5. PyRUA-Dex: Learning Programmatic Policies for Dexterous Bimanual Manipulation with Coding Agents
**Upvotes:** 👍 24 | **arXiv ID:** `2611.33144`

**Links:** [arXiv Page](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33144) | [Download PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.33144.pdf) | [Hugging Face Discussion](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.33144)

### Abstract:
> Learning for dexterous manipulation is limited by the scarcity of high-quality demonstrations: retargeting from human video is imprecise, teleoperation gives no force feedback to the operator, and exoskeleton-based collection is hard to scale up. We present PyRUA-Dex, a framework that enables coding agents to develop dexterous manipulation through programmatic policy, without the need of demonstrations. The programmatic policy is developed in two stages: during exploration, multiple agents explore in parallel for task skills and ways to compose them, and a distilling agent condenses the successful attempts into a task library and one program. During refinement, this program is improved through randomized layouts for enhanced robustness and execution efficiency. In simulated environments, PyRUA-Dex achieves SOTA on Bench2Dex with a score of 72.9 and reaches 81.0% success on DexJoCo under full randomization, ahead of every trained policy on both benchmarks. In real world experiments on a bimanual platform with two 22-DoF hands, PyRUA-Dex learns to play piano, write calligraphy, and other tasks efficiently: learning a new piece from sheet music takes as little as 30 minutes. These results suggest that PyRUA-Dex is an effective framework for developing dexterous manipulation policies with coding agents in the absence of high quality dexterous manipulation demonstrations.

## 6. ICLWorld: Benchmarking In-Context Learning of Agents from Digital to Physical Worlds
**Upvotes:** 👍 20 | **arXiv ID:** `2611.32649`

**Links:** [arXiv Page](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.32649) | [Download PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.32649.pdf) | [Hugging Face Discussion](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.32649)

### Abstract:
> In-context learning (ICL) lets agents acquire new rules from context and adapt to user-specific requirements at inference time. The same frontier agents now operate both computers and robots, yet their ICL remains insufficiently assessed: existing benchmarks are largely offline, rely on text or static images, and risk training-data contamination, while robot evaluations are limited to method-level ablations. We present ICLWorld, an online benchmark that evaluates the ICL of agents from digital to physical worlds, covering computer use and robot use, together with UIverse, a unified harness that turns every environment into a user interface. We build 30 environments, record 630 demonstration videos (10+ hours), and design 160 tasks with customized, often counter-intuitive rules that cannot be satisfied without learning from the context. Each task comes with three rule versions, given as text or video, and we test generalization at three levels, from replaying the demonstration to new instances and unseen environments. Across six frontier models, the strongest agents, Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra, succeed on 82% and 76% of computer-use runs but only 27% and 26% of robot-use runs, and a no-rule control reduces success to at most 8%. GPT-6 Astra succeeds under all three rule versions on only 38% of computer-use tasks, half its rate under a single rule, and answers 98% of robot rule questions correctly yet completes only 20% of these tasks. Frontier agents learn rules comparably well from video and from text and transfer them across environments, whereas weaker agents rely on text and lose most of their video-based success once the demonstrated situation is resampled. These findings reveal substantial gaps between the digital and the physical world and between identifying a rule and acting on it.

## 7. CoT-Proxy: Cross-Format Transfer for Distilling Long-Horizon Policies
**Upvotes:** 👍 14 | **arXiv ID:** `2611.33148`

**Links:** [arXiv Page](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.33148) | [Download PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.33148.pdf) | [Hugging Face Discussion](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.33148)

### Abstract:
> Reasoning models become long-horizon agents through reinforcement learning or, more cheaply, through fine-tuning on a stronger teacher's trajectories, which contain the teacher's chain-of-thought as well as its actions. The standard fine-tuning approach imitates both, yet the strongest teachers are closed models whose chain-of-thought is hidden behind an API. We show that the chain-of-thought can be left out altogether and that the long-horizon policy can be distilled from the teacher's actions alone. The student is trained on these trajectories with its reasoning block left empty, while a KL loss on a replay buffer of its own generations limits drift from its initial model. Surprisingly, the policy learned from these trajectories with an empty reasoning block carries over to inference with thinking enabled, where the student reasons in its own style while acting like the teacher, an effect we call cross-format transfer. Distilling the actions of Qwen3.6-27B raises the task success of Qwen3-4B on AppWorld from 7% to 18% and that of Qwen3-32B from 12% to 25%. We further introduce CoT-proxies, short summaries of the teacher's reasoning placed after the empty reasoning block, which raise Qwen3-4B further, to 35%. Since neither actions nor CoT-proxies need the hidden chain-of-thought, the recipe applies unchanged to closed teachers. Distilling GPT-5.6 Luna from its visible outputs and the reasoning summaries its API returns raises Qwen3-4B from 7% to 48% on AppWorld and from 3% to 28% on SWE-bench Verified.

## 8. Improving and Expanding Video Editing Capabilities Without Videos
**Upvotes:** 👍 13 | **arXiv ID:** `2611.34941`

**Links:** [arXiv Page](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34941) | [Download PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.34941.pdf) | [Hugging Face Discussion](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.34941)

### Abstract:
> Image editing models can follow a wide range of instructions, but video editing models still lag behind, limited in part by the difficulty of collecting paired source and edited videos. Is it possible to transfer advanced image editing capabilities to video editors without new paired videos? We introduce Image-to-Video Transfer (I2VT), which improves pretrained video editors and expands them to new editing tasks using an image editor. It enables efficient post-training with no architectural changes and no new videos, using only individual images. Our key idea is to let the video editor role-play as an image editor: it edits an image as a one-frame video, with the image editor guiding the video editor's predictions at each denoising step. Although the video editor sees only single images during training, at test time it applies the improved and expanded capabilities to whole video clips while preserving temporal consistency. To push the boundaries of video editing, we introduce OpenVE++, an extension of OpenVE-Bench that adds four new editing tasks useful for video production, including action changes, expression changes, body movements, and scene relighting. Our experiments across three different video editors show that I2VT improves existing editing capabilities and expands them to new tasks, and our models achieve state-of-the-art (SOTA) performance on both OpenVE-Bench and OpenVE++.

## 9. Target-Part Preference Optimization for Part-Aware 3D Generation
**Upvotes:** 👍 13 | **arXiv ID:** `2611.30472`

**Links:** [arXiv Page](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.30472) | [Download PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.30472.pdf) | [Hugging Face Discussion](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.30472)

### Abstract:
> Part-aware 3D generation requires not only globally plausible objects but also complete, well-separated semantic parts, yet high-quality part decompositions remain too scarce to support large-scale supervised fine-tuning. We study whether preference fine-tuning based on Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) can adapt a pretrained part-aware generator from limited part-annotated data without architectural changes. We rank candidates generated with different random seeds against ground-truth (GT) geometric metrics, and select the (object, target-part) examples with the largest across-seed quality gap. Each selected example stores a seed pair whose two flow trajectories share one clean GT compositional latent and differ only in Gaussian noise. We derive a reference-relative target-block preference loss from FlowDPO that compares the two trajectories against a frozen reference model. GT flow-matching losses on the target block and complete part set provide absolute supervision, while only the diffusion transformer (DiT) is updated. Human-ranked controls test an alternative ordering source, and synthetic cropping provides controlled loser targets that are incomplete by construction. Among the three training-set scales, the medium-scale setting gives the strongest result on held-out Objaverse and outperforms both the pretrained generator and the reported direct supervised fine-tuning baseline on the multi-part aggregate. Further analysis shows sensitivity to pair ordering, target-part coverage, and the balance between pairwise and GT supervision. Cross-dataset experiments show less consistent gains under distribution shift.

## 10. SpecEngineeringBench: Evaluating Coding Agents as Autonomous Specification Engineers
**Upvotes:** 👍 13 | **arXiv ID:** `2611.32973`

**Links:** [arXiv Page](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.32973) | [Download PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.32973.pdf) | [Hugging Face Discussion](https://huggingface.co/papers/2611.32973)

### Abstract:
> Autonomous specification engineering requires coding agents to translate functional requirements into executable tests and use the results to discover and validate defects. However, existing benchmarks evaluate coding agents on explicitly specified test generation tasks, commonly providing an issue description, a designated test entry point, or files to inspect, leaving the ability to determine what to test and where to investigate insufficiently evaluated. Relying on such clues bypasses the challenge of mapping requirements to implementations and proactively uncovering unknown defects. We propose SpecEngineeringBench, comprising 100 instances from 78 open-source Python repositories across 16 application categories, with multiple recorded defects in each instance. Two independent tasks evaluate complementary capabilities: generating test suites and discovering defects through execution analysis. The evaluation combines test validity, defect detection, and coverage with localization precision and recall. Across seven frontier coding agents, results reveal that test validity and coverage decouple from defect exposure: agents achieve a 91.47% Head Pass Rate and 80.48% Change-focused Coverage alongside a 26.36% Fail-to-Pass Rate, while localization recall drops from 78.11% at the file level to 51.29% at the function level. Autonomous test generation improves file, function, and hunk localization recall by 4.99, 2.77, and 2.69 percentage points over relying solely on existing repository test suites.
